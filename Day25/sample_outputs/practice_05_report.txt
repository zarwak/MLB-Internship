On these images ORB is 2.5x faster than Harris on the mean
(24 vs 18 ms on the median), which is not the
result the usual 'Harris is the cheap one' summary predicts. Two
things explain it:
  - ORB works to a fixed budget. nfeatures=1000 caps its work no
    matter how busy the image is.
  - Harris has no budget. It returns everything above the
    threshold, and the per-corner steps after the response map
    (connected components, then cornerSubPix on every centroid)
    scale with that count.

The clearest case is pair04_aloe_plant_a: 5066 corners past the
threshold and 264 ms spent, against ORB's capped 1000 in 45 ms.

The 'R map' column is the response map alone, without the
thresholding and sub-pixel steps: 18.3 ms on average, 2.9x cheaper
than full Harris and 1.2x cheaper than ORB. So the
Harris *operator* is indeed the cheap one; turning its output into
a usable corner list is what costs, and that cost is unbounded.

Read this table against the repeatability one above. Under
'rotate 90' Harris *finds* the corners again perfectly well, yet
almost none of them match. Those two facts together locate the
failure precisely: it is not the detector losing the corners, it is
the descriptor. A Harris corner has no orientation, so BRIEF lays
its sampling pattern down at a fixed angle and reads a rotated
patch in the wrong order. ORB rotates the pattern by the keypoint's
own angle first, which is the entire 'Rotated BRIEF' half of the
name. Detector and descriptor fail independently, and only the
pair of experiments tells you which one you are looking at.

==============================================================================
CONCLUSION  (from the numbers above, not from the textbook)
==============================================================================
1. Speed did not go the way the usual summary claims. The Harris
   response map is by far the cheapest thing here, but a full
   Harris corner list is not, because nothing bounds how many
   corners it has to refine. ORB's fixed feature budget makes its
   cost predictable, which matters more in practice than raw ms.

2. Rotation: Harris' response R is rotation invariant in theory -
   it is built from the eigenvalues of M, which do not depend on
   the window's orientation. Measured, it still fell to 46% at 45
   degrees against ORB's 80%. The theory is about the operator; the
   loss comes from everything around it - a square window, discrete
   Sobel kernels and the interpolation the warp itself introduces.
   Note rotate 90 scores much higher than rotate 45 for both, since
   a quarter turn needs no interpolation at all.

3. Scale is the real divide. At half size Harris repeatability was
   34% against ORB's 84%, and good matches fell to 16 against
   260. Harris has one fixed window and cannot see a corner that
   has changed size; ORB's pyramid is built precisely for this.

4. Only ORB can match on its own. The Harris column in experiment 3
   exists only because ORB's descriptor was bolted onto Harris'
   corners. Harris by itself outputs positions and stops.

So: Harris for corners in one image at one scale - tracking,
calibration targets, sub-pixel refinement. ORB when two images have
to be put into correspondence, which is what this project needs.
